1 · Evaluation of Credit Policy — the Method

Debtors (Receivables) Management — "should we relax/tighten the credit policy?"

The one question every variant is really asking

Every "evaluate the credit policy" question — relaxing credit period, allowing cash discount, comparing customers, factoring vs no factoring — reduces to one comparison:

Net Benefit = (Incremental Profit from the change) − (Incremental Cost of carrying the extra investment in debtors)

If Net Benefit is positive → adopt the change. If comparing multiple options (A vs B vs C, or Present vs Proposed), pick the option with the highest Net Benefit — not the highest sales, not the highest profit alone.

Step-by-step

  1. Identify the options being compared. Usually "Present Policy" vs "Proposed Policy", or Customer A vs B vs C at different credit periods (0/30/60/90 days). Set up one column per option.
  2. Compute Sales for each option. If sales rise with a longer credit period, use the new (higher) sales figure for that option only.
  3. Compute Contribution = Sales − Variable Cost. (Variable cost % stays the same across options unless stated otherwise.)
  4. Deduct additional Fixed Costs — only if the question says fixed costs increase because of the new policy (e.g. new credit department). If fixed costs are unchanged, ignore this line entirely — a common trap is deducting the same fixed cost in every column when it hasn't actually changed.
  5. Deduct Bad Debts = Sales × bad debt %. This % often changes between options (e.g. 1.5% → 2.5%) — always re-check which % applies to which column.
  6. Deduct Cash Discount (only if the policy includes an early-payment discount, e.g. "2/15 net 60") = Sales × discount % × proportion of customers expected to avail it.
  7. Arrive at PBT, deduct tax → PAT. This is "Profit (A)" for each option.
  8. Compute the Opportunity Cost of the investment in debtors — separately, as "(B)". This is the step almost everyone gets wrong (see the box below).
  9. Net Benefit = PAT (A) − Opportunity Cost (B) for each option.
  10. Compare across options and recommend the one with the highest Net Benefit. If it's a single present-vs-proposed comparison, recommend adopting the proposed policy only if its Net Benefit > the present policy's.
The #1 trap — how to value the investment in debtors (Step 8).
Do not use the sales value of debtors. Investment in debtors is valued at cost, because that is the actual money the firm has tied up — the profit margin inside the receivable hasn't been "spent" yet.
Opportunity Cost = Total Cost of Credit Sales × Collection period (days)360 (or 365) × Required Rate of Return

"Total Cost of Credit Sales" = Credit Sales − Contribution (i.e. Variable Cost + apportioned Fixed Cost, or simply Sales × (1 − contribution %) depending on what the question gives). If the question separately gives "cost of sales" or "total cost", use that figure directly instead of re-deriving it.

Other recurring variants of the same chapter

(a) Factoring evaluation

Compare the cost of factoring against the savings it brings, then compare the net cost of factoring (as a %) against the cost of alternative finance (e.g. bank overdraft):

Annual Cost of Factoring = Factoring commission + Interest charged by factor on advance
Savings on Factoring = Credit administration cost saved + Bad debts avoided
Net Cost = Annual Cost − Savings    Effective Cost % = Net CostAmount of advance actually made available × 100

If the effective cost % is lower than the bank's rate → avail factoring. If higher → don't.

(b) Multiple customers at multiple credit periods (matrix questions)

Build one table with a column per customer per credit period offered (0/30/60/90 days). For each cell compute: Sales → Contribution → Debtors (at cost, using average investment = Contribution period × Sales/360, or Debtors at cost = Cost × period/360) → Cost of carrying debtors → (Contribution − Cost of carrying). Pick, for each customer separately, the credit period where this net figure is highest.

Example 2 — Matrix of customers × credit periods (MTP1, Q2a)

Bright Vision Electronics: 3 customers A, B, C offered different sales quantities at 0/30/60/90-day credit. Selling price ₹9,000/TV; contribution 20% of selling price; cost of carrying receivables 20% p.a.; 360-day year.

Method used: for each customer, at each credit period, compute: Sales value → Contribution (20%) → Debtors at cost (i.e. 80% of sales value, since contribution is 20%) × period/360 → Cost of carrying those debtors (× 20%) → Excess of contribution over cost of carrying debtors. Customer A showed no sales increase with credit, so no benefit — leave at 0 days. For B and C, the excess was highest at 90 days for both.

Conclusion: allow 90-day credit to B and C; A stays at 0 days. Also flag downstream problems: A may now demand parity credit since C already gets 60 days; B may demand even longer credit (e.g. 120 days) since it buys more than C at the same 90-day term.

The extra twist this question tests — creditor offset
Average Debtors = Cost of Sales × period/360 (e.g. Policy A: 21,50,000 × 45/360 = 2,68,750). But since creditors give 15 extra days on the incremental variable cost, that portion is already financed by suppliers — so it's deducted before applying the required return: Average Creditors for extra VC = incremental VC × 15/360 (Policy A: 1,50,000 × 15/360 = 6,250). Net investment in receivables = Average Debtors − Average Creditors (Policy A: 2,68,750 − 6,250 = 2,62,500) → × 20% = ₹52,500 opportunity cost. Skipping this offset is the single most common mistake in this question type.

Recommendation: Policy A (45 days) — highest Net Benefit of ₹10,64,500.

W.N. — who funds what, and at what rate (the part everyone finds confusing)
In-house interest cost (baseline being replaced) = Credit sales × collection period/360 × OD rate = 75,00,000 × 60/360 × 10% = ₹1,25,000.
Under factoring: Debtors at the new 30-day period = 75,00,000 × 30/360 = ₹6,25,000. Factor reserve (10%) = ₹62,500 held back; factor commission for the period (1.5% of debtors) = ₹9,375 — both reduce what's actually financed. Net advance base = 6,25,000 − 62,500 − 9,375 = ₹5,53,125. Factor actually advances 80% of this = ₹4,42,500, on which interest @12% = ₹53,100 (this is cost B). The remaining ₹1,82,500 (6,25,000 − 4,42,500) is not funded by the factor at all — Sukrut still finances it itself via overdraft @10% = ₹18,250.
Net savings in interest = old in-house cost 1,25,000 − new overdraft-only cost 18,250 = ₹1,06,750 (this goes into savings A; the ₹53,100 factor interest is a separate cost in B — don't double-count either figure on both sides).

Advice: Savings (₹1,89,250) exceed cost (₹1,65,600) → accept the factoring proposal (net benefit ₹23,650/year).

4 · How Arbitrage Works (Capital Structure — MM Approach)

Modigliani-Miller, without-tax world — the mechanism that forces Value(Levered) = Value(Unlevered)

The one-line idea

MM's without-tax proposition says the value of a firm is independent of its capital structure — a levered firm and an unlevered firm with the same business risk and same EBIT must have the same total value. Arbitrage is the market mechanism MM use to prove this: if the two firms are ever mispriced relative to each other, an investor can costlessly exploit the gap by switching their personal portfolio — buying into the underpriced firm and selling the overpriced one — until the prices converge and the arbitrage profit disappears.

The key trick: since the firm's debt and the investor's personal borrowing are treated as perfect substitutes (this is called "personal" or "homemade" leverage), an investor can replicate or undo the company's leverage on their own account — so it should never matter to firm value whether the leverage sits inside the company or in the investor's own pocket.

Why arbitrage exists — the setup

Two firms, identical EBIT & business risk
→
One is Levered (debt + equity)
Other is Unlevered (all-equity)
Value of Levered firm, VL = SL + D    where SL = EBIT − InterestKe (Levered)
Value of Unlevered firm, VU = SU    where SU = EBITKe (Unlevered)

If VL ≠ VU, MM says this can't be a stable equilibrium — investors will arbitrage the difference away.

5 · Arbitrage — Step-by-Step Mechanics

Two mirror-image cases: overvalued Levered firm, or overvalued Unlevered firm

Case A — Value of Levered firm > Value of Unlevered firm

(The more commonly tested case.) An investor holding x% of the Levered firm's equity does the following:

  1. Sell the shares held in the Levered company → realises x% × SL (Value of Levered firm's equity).
  2. Borrow personally an amount = x% × D (the same proportion of the levered firm's debt) — at the same interest rate the company itself pays. This recreates, in the investor's own hands, the exact same personal financial risk they gave up by selling the levered shares.
  3. Invest the total (sale proceeds + personal borrowing) into shares of the Unlevered company.
  4. Compute new income: Income from Unlevered shares (at KeU) minus Interest on the personal loan (at the borrowing rate).
  5. Compare with the old income the investor was earning as a Levered shareholder (x% × Earnings to Levered equity).
  6. The difference is a risk-free arbitrage gain — same personal risk as before (because Step 2 recreated it), but higher income. Every investor holding Levered shares has an incentive to do this.

As more investors do this: demand for Levered shares falls → its price (and VL) falls; demand for Unlevered shares rises → its price (and VU) rises. The process continues until VL = VU, at which point the arbitrage gain becomes zero and there's no more incentive to switch — equilibrium is restored.

Case B — Value of Unlevered firm > Value of Levered firm (mirror image)

  1. Sell the shares held in the Unlevered company.
  2. Buy shares of the Levered company in the same proportion the investor wants of both debt and equity claims — effectively lend part of the proceeds (buy a slice of the levered firm's debt, or simply invest a matching proportion in the levered firm's equity while treating part of the outlay as replicating the debt side) so the personal risk profile stays unchanged.
  3. Because the levered firm is cheaper per rupee of EBIT claimed, the same total investment now buys a larger income stream.
  4. Compare new income (from levered equity, at KeL, on the smaller equity outlay) with the old income from unlevered shares — again a risk-free gain.

This selling pressure on Unlevered shares and buying pressure on Levered shares pushes VU down and VL up until they meet.

Exam checklist for an arbitrage question:
  1. Compute SL, VL, SU, VU first — always.
  2. Identify which firm is overvalued.
  3. Sell shares in the overvalued firm (× investor's holding %).
  4. Borrow/lend an amount that keeps the investor's personal financial risk unchanged — this is the step students most often skip. Without it, the "arbitrage" isn't riskless and the comparison is invalid.
  5. Buy shares in the undervalued firm with the total funds.
  6. Compute new income − interest (if borrowed) → compare to old income → state the incremental gain.

Why the personal risk is unchanged
Before: the investor held levered equity — risk came from the company's own ₹1,50,000 debt (of which the investor's share is 20%, i.e. ₹30,000-worth of exposure). After: the investor sold that equity but personally borrowed the exact same ₹30,000 at the same 10% — so the investor is carrying the identical amount of leverage, just relocated from "inside the company" to "on their own account." That is exactly why the ₹750 gain is a free lunch, not a reward for taking more risk — and exactly why MM say this can't persist: everyone will do it until VL falls and VU rises enough to erase the ₹750.

The one-line takeaway examiners want
Without tax: leverage changes nothing — VL = VU and WACC stays at Ko (18%) regardless of debt, because there's no tax shield to capture.
With tax: leverage adds value — VL = VU + (Debt × tax rate); the more debt, the higher the firm's value and the lower its WACC (18% → 13.23% here), because interest is tax-deductible.

Instead of computing "extra income," fix the investment at the same amount the investor originally had (12% of Unlevered firm's total value = 12% × 2,00,000 = ₹24,000) and see how much cash is left over:
Money in hand from selling + borrowing = 18,240 + 9,000 = ₹27,240. Only ₹24,000 of that is needed to buy the equivalent 12% stake in the Unlevered firm → ₹3,240 surplus cash is left over.

So the investor ends up with the same income as a Levered shareholder plus ₹3,240 free cash — a cleaner way to see the same ₹405-a-year-equivalent gain (₹3,240 one-off ≈ capitalised value of the ₹405 annual gain at 12.5%: 405/0.125 = 3,240).
